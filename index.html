Your first guide
to yoga.

If you've been meaning to try yoga for a while (maybe longer than you'd like to admit), this is where you start. Eleven poses, nine classes, and answers to the questions you'd feel a bit awkward asking on day one.

From Harriet King, founder and teacher, WellNest Kirkstall

The poses you'll meet

These are the shapes that appear in almost every WellNest class. Select a pose to see its details, where you'll feel it, and (most importantly) what to do if it feels like too much.

Mountain Pose

Tadasana
What it is
You stand. Feet together or hip-width apart, arms at your sides, weight evenly through both feet. The foundation almost everything else grows from.
Where you feel it
Feet, legs, and core, particularly if you actually engage them. Most people stand in Mountain Pose without noticing how much is going on.
What it's good for
Posture and body awareness. It teaches you how you actually hold yourself, and that knowledge carries into the rest of your day.
Beginner note
There's nowhere to go wrong here. Breathe. That's it.

Child's Pose

Balasana
What it is
You kneel, fold your torso forward over your thighs, and rest your forehead on the mat. Arms stretch forward or rest by your sides.
Where you feel it
Lower back, hips, and shoulders. One of the most genuinely restorative shapes in yoga.
What it's good for
Rest. Regulation. A pause. The nervous system takes it as a signal to slow down. Teachers at WellNest will offer it regularly, so you can take it whenever you need it.
Beginner note
At WellNest, Child's Pose is always available. "Child's pose is welcome at any time." The teachers mean it, every single class.

Downward-Facing Dog

Adho Mukha Svanasana
What it is
Hands and feet on the mat, hips lifted high so your body forms an inverted V. You'll do this in almost every vinyasa class.
Where you feel it
Backs of the legs, calves, shoulders, and wrists. A lot is happening at once, which is exactly why it's used so often.
What it's good for
Full-body stretch and mild inversion. Decompresses the spine and brings fresh blood to the head. Teachers often use it as a rest between sequences.
Beginner note
Bent knees are completely fine. You're trying to lengthen your spine, not get your heels on the floor. Those are different things.

Cobra Pose

Bhujangasana
What it is
Lie face down, hands under your shoulders, lift your chest off the mat. Elbows stay slightly bent.
Where you feel it
Lower back and chest opening. For people who spend the day at a desk, this is one of the most important shapes in the practice.
What it's good for
Counteracting the forward-fold of a working day. Strengthens the back, opens the front body.
Beginner note
Low Cobra (lifting just a few inches) is often more useful than a big backbend. Come up only as far as your back invites you.

Warrior I

Virabhadrasana I
What it is
A lunge with the back foot turned out at 45 degrees, hips facing forward, arms lifted overhead. Strong. Grounded.
Where you feel it
Front thigh, hip flexors, and through the whole back leg. You'll feel your core working to keep the hips square.
What it's good for
Leg strength and hip flexibility. A satisfying pose to land in once you find your balance.
Beginner note
Don't worry about perfect alignment on day one. Find stability first. The refinement comes over weeks, not minutes.

Warrior II

Virabhadrasana II
What it is
Like Warrior I, but your hips and arms open wide to the sides, front knee bent over front ankle, gaze over your front fingers.
Where you feel it
Inner thighs, outer hips, and shoulders. It looks powerful because it is.
What it's good for
Strengthening the legs, opening the hips, building stamina. Common in Vinyasa and Hatha classes.
Beginner note
Check that your front knee tracks over your second toe, not collapsing inward. Your teacher will cue this.

Cat / Cow

Marjaryasana / Bitilasana
What it is
On hands and knees, you alternate between arching your back downward (Cow, inhale) and rounding it up toward the ceiling (Cat, exhale). One of the first things you'll do in most classes.
Where you feel it
The whole length of the spine, from tailbone to neck. Very satisfying if you've been sitting all day.
What it's good for
Waking up spinal mobility, linking breath to movement, warming up the body gently before anything demanding.
Beginner note
Move slowly. There's no version of this that's wrong if it feels good in your back.

Tree Pose

Vrksasana
What it is
Stand on one foot, place the sole of the other foot against your inner calf or thigh (never the knee), arms rise or press at heart centre.
Where you feel it
The standing foot and ankle, inner thigh of the bent leg, core. Balance poses are deceptively tiring.
What it's good for
Balance, focus, and the kind of concentration that leaves no room for anything else.
Beginner note
Wobbling is fine: it's a sign your stabilisers are working. Keep a soft gaze on one spot on the floor.

Seated Forward Fold

Paschimottanasana
What it is
Sit with legs extended, then hinge from the hips to fold forward. Hands go wherever they reach: feet, shins, or a strap.
Where you feel it
Hamstrings, lower back, and calves. If you sit at a desk, these will be tight. Entirely normal.
What it's good for
Deep hamstring release and calming the nervous system. Core of most yin and restorative classes.
Beginner note
Bent knees are fine. A strap around your feet is good technique. Fold from the hips, not the middle of your back.

Pigeon Pose

Eka Pada Rajakapotasana
What it is
From Downward Dog, one knee comes forward toward the same-side wrist, the back leg extends behind. You can stay upright or fold forward over the front leg.
Where you feel it
Outer hip of the front leg, and hip flexor of the back. Many people find this intense. That's by design.
What it's good for
Deep hip release. If you carry stress in your hips (which most people do), Pigeon is where you'll feel it.
Beginner note
Support your hip with a blanket if it doesn't reach the floor. Sharp pain means stop. A deep, sustained ache means stay and breathe into it.

Savasana

Corpse Pose
What it is
You lie flat on your back, arms slightly away from your sides, eyes closed, and do nothing. Every class ends here. It lasts 5–10 minutes.
Where you feel it
Everywhere and nowhere. The body settles. Many people find this the hardest part, particularly in the first few classes.
What it's good for
Integration. The nervous system needs time to absorb what the practice has done. Skipping Savasana is like having a bath and not towelling dry.
Beginner note
If lying still feels impossible, that's completely normal. It gets easier. Your mind will wander. Just notice it and come back.

Before your first class

Everything you need to know so that when you walk in, nothing feels unfamiliar.

What to bring

  • 🧘A mat, or don't: WellNest provides mats, bolsters, blocks, blankets, and straps. You need to bring nothing.
  • 💧Water, a bottle if you have one. There's a chill-out area to leave things.
  • 👕Something comfortable to move in, loose or fitted, whatever you own. No specialist kit required.
  • 🧦Socks optional, yoga is usually barefoot, but bring socks if your feet get cold.
  • 🍽️A light stomach, eat something small at least an hour before. A full meal right before class is uncomfortable.

What to expect when you arrive

  • 📍WellNest is at Chantry House, Victoria Road, Kirkstall LS5 3JB, in a beautifully converted church. You'll know it when you see it.
  • ⏰Arrive 5 to 10 minutes early on your first visit. It gives you time to find a mat, settle in, and have a word with the teacher.
  • 👟Shoes off before you enter the studio space. Leave your things in the chill-out area.
  • 🙋Tell the teacher it's your first time. You don't have to, but if you do, they'll offer modifications and keep an eye on you.
  • 📵Phone on silent. One hour without it won't hurt.

The things people worry about

"I'm not flexible enough."

This is the thing Harriet hears most often, and it's the wrong way round. Flexibility is what yoga gives you. It's not what you arrive with. Nobody in the room started flexible. Come as you are.

"I'll be the only beginner."

You won't be. And even if you were, it wouldn't matter. The class moves at its own pace. WellNest has no mirrors. Nobody is watching you.

"What if I can't keep up?"

There's no keeping up. The class meets you where you are. If something is too much, Child's Pose is always there. The teachers mean it every time they say it.

"I keep starting and stopping. What if I quit again?"

Then you come back. WellNest has members who quit at month three, came back at month nine, and are now in their fourth year. There's no starting over here. You just continue from wherever you are.

The one rule that covers everything else: Child's Pose is always an option. Always. No explanation needed.
